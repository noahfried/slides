### 21. How did Republicans respond?

- Facing bad polls, Trump and the Republicans tried to **change the rules** before November
- **Supporters said:** the new rules would stop non-citizens from voting and prevent fraud
- **Critics said:** non-citizen voting is extremely rare; the real goal was to make voting harder and **tilt the election**
- Three main strategies:
  1. New voting laws (the SAVE America Act)
  2. Limiting mail-in voting
  3. Redrawing district maps

### 22. New voting rules

**The SAVE America Act**
- Would have required **proof of citizenship** (e.g. a passport) to register and a **photo ID** to vote
- Passed the House in February, 218–213
- Never passed the Senate: not enough votes to beat the **filibuster**
- Trump demanded that the Senate abolish the filibuster; Republican leaders said they didn't have the votes

**Trump's mail-in voting order**
- Signed March 31, 2026
- The Postal Service would have **refused to deliver** mail ballots to anyone not on a government-approved list
- September: the Supreme Court blocked it for 2026, 7–2
- Mail voting went ahead as usual

Source: Sources: NBC News; Votebeat; The Hill.

NOTE: The 7–2 count is from the original deck. Votebeat confirms the block and two dissenters (Alito and Thomas).

### 36. The map war: what actually happened

Republicans planned **up to +15 seats** in seven states. Instead:

- **Texas:** hoped for a 30–8 delegation and got 24–14. Won only 1 of 5 target seats and lost 2 seats they already held
- **Florida:** targeted Democrats held on in several redrawn seats, including Kathy Castor. Some races are still being counted
- **Missouri:** voters rejected the Republican map in a referendum (Prop A); Rep. Emanuel Cleaver won re-election
- **New word: dummymander.** A gerrymander that spreads your voters too thin, so it backfires in a bad year

Source: Texas Tribune; CBS News; KCUR; Mediaite, as of Nov. 4.

NOTE: I titled this neutrally rather than "backfired", though the Texas Tribune's own headline uses that word. "Dummymander" is political slang, which your students might enjoy.

NOTE: Missouri isn't on slide 24's chart because its new map was used in the August primary but not in November. Florida's 4-seat target hasn't been settled, and at least FL-7, 9, 13 and 27 were uncalled. That's why I didn't write "0 of 4".

### 37. Did money decide it? (OPTIONAL)

- The most expensive election cycle ever: about **$11.6 billion** in political ads
- Texas Senate: the most expensive non-presidential race on record (over **$573 million**)
- Elon Musk spent at least **$474 million**, $224 million of it in Texas
- Outside groups spent about **$300M** for Paxton and **$80M** for Talarico. Talarico won by 12
- Michigan too: about **$169M** for Rogers, **$94M** for El-Sayed
- Remember Stevens, who outspent El-Sayed 9 to 1?

Source: AdImpact via Al Jazeera, The Hill and others (late Oct.); CNBC analysis of campaign finance records (Nov. 4).

NOTE: Spending numbers come from different dates and sources, so they aren't strictly comparable. One nuance for discussion: Talarico himself raised far more than Paxton ($146M vs. about $22M), so "outside money" isn't the whole picture.

### 39. Governors and ballot measures (OPTIONAL)

**Governors**
- Democrats flipped **5**: Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio, Texas
- Democrats clinched a majority of governorships for the first time in at least 16 years
- Texas Gov. Greg Abbott lost to Gina Hinojosa, 52.9–45.6
- Keisha Lance Bottoms (Georgia): first Black woman elected governor in U.S. history
- Kansas and Alaska: still uncalled

**Ballot measures: voters didn't simply follow party lines**
- **Abortion rights** won in Virginia and Nevada; Missouri rejected a repeal
- But Idaho rejected an abortion-rights measure
- **Voter ID** passed in Ohio, North Carolina and Nevada, but failed in California
- **Redistricting:** Maryland approved an amendment that could allow a new map for 2028

Source: NBC News; France 24/AFP; Stateline, as of Nov. 4.

NOTE: Sources disagree on the governors "first since" line: 16 years (NBC) vs. two decades (AP). "At least 16 years" is true under both.

NOTE: Kansas is Democratic-held: Holscher 50.3–49.7, 98% counted. The Abbott race isn't explicitly labeled projected in NBC's table, but other outlets report he lost.

### 40. Why did it happen? (OPTIONAL)

**Democrats say**
- A "change election" (Hakeem Jeffries)
- Voters want action on costs, health care and accountability

**Republicans say**
- The President's party usually loses seats at the midterm (Mike Johnson)
- "I wasn't on the Ballot" (Trump); a White House official blamed the quality of candidates

**What the exit polls show**
- 6 in 10 said Trump was a factor in their vote
- Voters disliked both parties: Democrats net −6, Republicans net −18

Source: CBS News; NBC News; RTÉ/AFP; Al Jazeera, Nov. 4.

### 41. What happens next?

- Trump stays President until **January 2029**: midterms cannot remove him
- The new Congress starts in **January 2027**. Until then, Republicans keep control (a "lame duck" Congress)
- Expected leaders: Hakeem Jeffries, first Black **Speaker of the House**; Chuck Schumer, **Senate Majority Leader**
- Democrats can investigate, and can block Trump's nominees and laws
- But Trump can **veto** bills, and overriding him takes two-thirds of each chamber
- The filibuster still means most bills need 60 Senate votes
- Trump says he accepts the results: "absolutely willing to accept" them

Source: NBC News; CBS News; The Hill; New York Times via RTÉ/AFP, Nov. 4.

NOTE: Jeffries and Schumer are "expected", not elected. Senate Democrats vote in early December, and some senators-elect have said they won't back Schumer.
