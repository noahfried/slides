## Section: Pandemics

### 5. The problem: pandemics
- A virus does not stop at a border
- COVID-19 reached almost every country within **three months**
- More than **7 million** reported deaths worldwide
- What happens if every country keeps its data to itself?
- Source: WHO COVID-19 dashboard

NOTE: The 7 million figure is WHO's count of *reported* deaths, from memory (it has been just over 7 million since 2024). WHO's own estimate of the real toll is much higher. "Three months" is my shorthand for first reports in late December 2019 to WHO's pandemic declaration on 11 March 2020.

### 6. World Health Organization (WHO)
- **Headquarters:** Geneva, Switzerland
- **Founded:** 7 April 1948 (now World Health Day)
- **Members:** 194 countries
- **Goal:** "the attainment by all peoples of the highest possible level of health"
- **US connection:** the US announced its withdrawal in January 2025 and says it left in January 2026
- Source: WHO Constitution, Article 1; HHS, Jan. 2026

NOTE: Small fix to your quote: the WHO Constitution says "all **peoples**," not "all people."

NOTE: The US status is disputed, so I worded it as "says it left." The US government treats the exit as complete (22 January 2026). In May 2026 a World Health Assembly committee took the position that the withdrawal isn't recognized until about $280 million in unpaid dues is settled, and agreed to suspend US voting rights from May 2027 if they stay unpaid. I could not confirm the final plenary vote. The 194 figure is WHO's long-standing count and still includes the US.

### 7. What does that mean?
- **Coordinates the global response** to outbreaks: collects data, declares emergencies, issues guidance
- **Sets health standards** that countries can adopt
  - Example (2025): "use of lower-sodium salt substitutes"
- **Runs long-term campaigns**, such as **MPOWER** against tobacco
- Source: WHO, *Use of lower-sodium salt substitutes* (Jan. 2025)

### 8. MPOWER: six steps against tobacco
- **M**onitor tobacco use and prevention policies
- **P**rotect people from tobacco smoke
- **O**ffer help to quit tobacco use
- **W**arn about the dangers of tobacco
- **E**nforce bans on tobacco advertising, promotion and sponsorship
- **R**aise taxes on tobacco
- Source: WHO, MPOWER (introduced 2008)

NOTE: The six letters are set as large red initials. Possible discussion hook: Austria only banned smoking in bars and restaurants in November 2019, one of the last countries in the EU to do so. I left it off the slide; say the word if you want it as a bullet.

### 9. The problem: air travel
- More than **100,000 flights** take off every day
- A plane built in the US, flown by an Austrian airline, lands in Japan
- On the way it crosses about **ten countries**: Austria → Hungary → Romania → Turkey → Georgia → Azerbaijan → Kazakhstan → China → South Korea → Japan
- Map on the right: the Vienna–Tokyo route as a red line across a grey world map, with the countries it crosses shaded
- Which country's rules apply? What language does the pilot speak to the tower?
- Source: ICAO; Austrian Airlines

The exact country list is my reconstruction of the usual southern route, not a tracked flight, and it varies daily with wind, so I wrote "about ten." If you want it exact, check a recent Vienna–Tokyo flight on Flightradar24 and send me the list. I also changed "built in France" to "built in the US," because Austrian uses Boeing 777s on this route.

NOTE: I changed your heading from "Air Traffic Control" to "Air Travel." ICAO does not control air traffic itself (national bodies do that, e.g. Austro Control in Austria, the FAA in the US). It writes the rules they all follow. "More than 100,000 flights a day" is ICAO's usual round figure and is from memory.

### 10. International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO)
- **Headquarters:** Montreal, Canada
- **Founded:** 4 April 1947
- **Members:** 193 countries
- **What it does:** sets shared standards for safety, pilot licences, airports and air traffic rules
- **US connection:** created by the Chicago Convention, signed in Chicago in 1944
- Source: ICAO

NOTE: 193 members is from memory; the search confirmed the dates and Montreal but not the count.

### 11. ICAO standards you have already met
- **English:** every pilot and controller on international flights must pass an English test (ICAO "Level 4")
- **The alphabet:** Alfa, Bravo, Charlie ... so that "B" and "D" are never confused over the radio
- **Your passport:** the same size in every country, with two lines of machine-readable code at the bottom
- **Black boxes:** every airliner must carry flight recorders, and crash reports are shared with all countries
- **Airport codes:** Vienna is **LOWW** to every pilot in the world

### 12. What if a country doesn't follow the rules?
- ICAO has **no power to punish** anyone. It can only inspect and publish the results
- But other countries can **close their airspace** to airlines they don't trust
- Example: in 2020, Pakistan found that almost a third of its pilots had fake or suspicious licences
- The EU **banned** Pakistan's national airline for more than four years (2020–2024)
- Source: EASA, Nov. 2024

NOTE: Slides 11 and 12 are new, per your instruction ("examples of standards and what happens if people don't follow them"). I moved English and the airport code here from the agency slide and dropped the VIE/IATA detail. The English Level 4 rule and the Pakistan case are confirmed by search; the phonetic alphabet, passport standard and flight recorders are well-known ICAO standards but from memory. If two slides is too much, slide 12 is the one I'd keep: it shows how rules work without a police force, which pays off in the AI discussion.

## Section: The Internet

### 13. The problem: staying connected
- Radio signals and satellites ignore borders
- Your phone works when you land in another country
- Every country has its own calling code: **+43** Austria, **+1** USA
- Who decides who gets which frequency, and which number?

### 14. International Telecommunication Union (ITU)
- **Headquarters:** Geneva, Switzerland
- **Founded:** 17 May 1865, as the International *Telegraph* Union: the oldest UN agency
- **Members:** 194 countries
- **What it does:** divides up radio frequencies and satellite orbits, sets technical standards, assigns country calling codes
  - Your laptop finds Wi-Fi in Vienna, New York and Tokyo, because Wi-Fi uses the **same frequencies** everywhere
  - A radio station in Vienna and one in Bratislava (60 km away) don't drown each other out
  - Every satellite, including Starlink's, has to be **registered** with the ITU
- **But:** it does *not* run the internet. Web addresses are managed by **ICANN**, a non-profit based in California
- **US connection:** its Secretary-General, Doreen Bogdan-Martin, is American 
- Source: ITU

NOTE: The three sub-bullets are the concrete examples you asked for. They are from memory (the search confirmed the satellite registration role, not the Wi-Fi band). Wi-Fi is "almost the same" everywhere rather than identical: a few channels differ by country. I kept the slide wording simple. This slide is now the fullest in the deck; if it looks crowded when built I'll move the examples to their own slide.

NOTE: Your heading says the ITU regulates the internet. That's only partly true, and it's a live political argument: at a 2012 ITU conference, a group of countries pushed for more government control of the internet through the ITU, and the US, UK and others refused to sign. I kept "The Internet" as the section name but added the "But" bullet. This also makes a nice bridge to AI (a global technology run mostly by private American organizations).

NOTE: Refresh before class. The ITU's leadership election is happening right now in Doha (conference 9–27 November 2026; Secretary-General vote scheduled from 12 November). Bogdan-Martin is running for re-election with US backing. As of 11 November there was no result.

## Section: Mail

### 15. The problem: sending a letter abroad
- You buy an Austrian stamp and post a letter to New York
- The Austrian post gets your money. The **US** post does the delivery
- Before 1874: a separate treaty between every pair of countries, and a different price for every route
- Who pays the American mail carrier?

NOTE: I changed "Shipping" to "Mail," because the UPU only covers postal services. If you meant cargo ships, that's a different agency: the International Maritime Organization (IMO, London). Tell me and I'll swap it.

### 16. Universal Postal Union (UPU)
- **Headquarters:** Bern, Switzerland
- **Founded:** 9 October 1874 (now World Post Day)
- **Members:** 192 countries
- **What it does:** makes the world "a single postal territory." Three rules:
  - **Deliver it like your own:** every member must deliver foreign mail as if it were domestic mail
  - **Freedom of transit:** every member must pass on mail that is only travelling through
  - **Terminal dues:** the sending country pays the delivering country a fixed fee, set by the UPU
- **US connection:** in 2018 the US announced it would leave, saying the fees made it too cheap to ship parcels from China. It stayed after the rules were changed in 2019
- Source: UPU Constitution, Article 1; Universal Postal Convention

NOTE: The three rules answer your question. The first two come from the UPU's Constitution and Convention (confirmed). On the third: for almost a century there was no fee at all. Each country simply kept the money from its own stamps, on the theory that every letter gets a reply, so it evens out. Terminal dues were only added in 1969, once it was clear that some countries receive far more mail than they send. That history is from memory and I couldn't confirm the 1969 date by search, so it's here for you to tell, not on the slide.

## Section: Nuclear Technology

### 17. The problem: nuclear technology
- The same technology makes **electricity** and **bombs**
- **Nine** countries have nuclear weapons: about **12,000** warheads
- A country says its nuclear program is only for energy. How does anyone know?
- Source: SIPRI Yearbook 2025

NOTE: I changed "Nuclear Weapons" to "Nuclear Technology." The IAEA doesn't regulate the weapons of the countries that already have them. It promotes peaceful nuclear use and inspects everyone else to check nothing is diverted to weapons. The 12,000 figure is SIPRI's January 2025 estimate (12,241), from memory.

### 18. International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)
- **Headquarters:** **Vienna**, Austria
- **Founded:** 29 July 1957
- **Members:** about 180 countries
- **What it does:** helps countries use nuclear technology peacefully, and sends **inspectors** to check that it stays peaceful
- Nobel Peace Prize, 2005
- **US connection:** proposed by President Eisenhower in his "Atoms for Peace" speech at the UN, 1953
- Image: the Vienna International Centre (UNO City)
- Source: IAEA

NOTE: Member count: I found 178 (a 2025 source) and 182 (Wikipedia, current); the IAEA's own list wouldn't load. "About 180" is safe. Possible irony to mention out loud: the world's nuclear agency sits in a country that voted against nuclear power in 1978 and never switched on its one plant (Zwentendorf). The IAEA has also been in the news over inspections in Iran; I left that off because it changes fast.
